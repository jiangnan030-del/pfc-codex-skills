# Theory

## Scope

This reference covers Chapter 5.4 and folds in the chapter-level takeaways from 5.5.

## Why the flat-joint model is introduced

The chapter presents the flat-joint model as a more brittle-rock-capable alternative to the standard BPM. It targets four issues:

- insufficient particle interlocking
- inadequate rotation resistance
- shear strength that does not respond appropriately to normal stress
- lack of built-in structural weakness such as prefabricated cracks

## Four mechanism upgrades

### 1. Increased self-locking effect

The model introduces an installation-gap concept so flat-joint contacts can be assigned in a wider but controlled neighborhood. This increases coordination and effective interlocking.

### 2. Appropriate rotation resistance

Flat-joint contacts are discretized into multiple elements across an abstract interface. Even when local failure occurs, the structural representation continues to provide a more realistic rotational resistance than a purely point-contact style bond.

### 3. Stress-dependent shear strength

Bonded flat-joint elements follow a Coulomb-type strength rule with tension cut-off, which better aligns shear resistance with normal stress level.

### 4. Prefabricated cracks and structural weakness

The chapter uses the model to study crack-density or structural-defect effects, especially in Brazilian-style tensile problems and discing-type failure patterns.

## Main application routes in the chapter

- uniaxial tension and triaxial compression
- fine-scale parameter calibration and matching
- Brazilian test analysis
- parameter influence on tensile microcracking
- core discing under different principal-stress states

## Chapter-level takeaway

The model is useful when the task needs brittle-rock fracture realism, parametric fracture studies, or discing-style structural interpretation that standard BPM cannot support well enough.
